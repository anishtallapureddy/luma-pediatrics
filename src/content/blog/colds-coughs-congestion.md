When your child is up coughing and no one is sleeping, it’s understandable to want something that will help right away. Parents often ask me what to buy or which cough medicine to use.

For most children, I start with a few things you may already have at home.

## Saline can help a stuffy nose

Plain saline drops or spray can loosen mucus. For babies, gentle suction afterward can make feeding easier. Try it before a breast or bottle feed if your baby is having trouble eating because of congestion.

Older children can use saline and then blow their nose. You don’t need to suction every time you hear mucus—focus on helping your child feed and rest comfortably.[^home-remedies]

## Keep offering fluids

Continue breast milk or formula for babies. Smaller, more frequent feeds may be easier when they’re congested. Offer older children drinks throughout the day.

For children 1 year and older, ½ to 1 teaspoon of honey may help a bothersome cough. Never give honey to babies younger than 12 months, because it can cause infant botulism.[^colds]

## Be careful with cough and cold medicines

Over-the-counter cough and cold medicines are not recommended for children younger than 4. Between ages 4 and 6, use them only if your child’s doctor recommends them.

For older children, follow the label’s age and dosing instructions. Check the ingredients before giving more than one product; combination medicines can contain ingredients you’ve already given separately.

If your child has a runny nose or mild cough but is comfortable, playing, and sleeping, you don’t need to treat every symptom.[^home-remedies]

## Expect gradual improvement

Many colds improve over 7–10 days. The mucus may become thicker or change color along the way.

Call your pediatrician if nasal symptoms last longer than 10–14 days, a daytime cough continues beyond 10 days, or your child develops ear pain. For babies 3 months or younger, call at the first sign of illness. Young babies can become sick quickly, and their symptoms may be subtle.[^children-colds]

## Watch how your child is breathing

This is one of the most important things I ask parents about.

Seek medical care promptly if your child is breathing much faster than usual, wheezing, or having trouble breathing even after you clear their nose.

If the skin between or beneath the ribs pulls inward with each breath, go to the emergency department. **Call 911** if your child is struggling for each breath, turns blue or gray around the lips or face, or cannot be awakened.[^breathing]

Also call if your child is getting worse or a fever returns after being gone for more than 24 hours.[^colds]

Poor drinking, fewer wet diapers or bathroom trips, a dry mouth, or unusual sleepiness are reasons to contact your pediatrician.[^dehydration] And a baby younger than 3 months with a temperature of **100.4°F (38°C) or higher** needs immediate medical attention, even if it seems like a cold.[^baby]

You don’t have to figure out which virus your child has before calling. Tell us what you’re seeing, what has changed, and what worries you. That’s a good place to start.

*This information is for general education and does not replace medical advice from your child’s healthcare provider. For a medical emergency, call 911.*
